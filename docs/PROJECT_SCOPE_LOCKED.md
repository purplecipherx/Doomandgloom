# Doomandgloom — Locked Scope and Expansion Plan

## Core goal

Map and investigate the overlapping ecosystem around doom/collapse/control narratives, alternative finance, paid forecasting, precious metals, health/technology products, conferences, subscriptions, and media amplification.

The project is designed to expose **documented misconduct, deceptive marketing, conflicts of interest, bad products, false or unsupported claims, failed forecasts, hidden commercial relationships, and monetization funnels** wherever the evidence supports those conclusions.

The project does **not** assume every connected person is bad. Connections are evidence of relationships, not guilt.

## Locked core requirements

These are now mandatory:

1. Minimum **50 distinct sources per investigated person/entity** before a final profile.
2. Relationship web with strength/type labels.
3. Money-only graph.
4. Product ownership/manufacturing/supply-chain graph.
5. Fear-to-commerce funnel analysis.
6. Securities/market-influence conflict analysis.
7. 50-item conflict-of-interest checklist.
8. External researcher/critic registry.
9. Prediction scorecards using dated pre-event material.
10. Plain-English final reporting.

# Additional high-value modules

## 1. Entity-resolution / hidden-company module

Track:
- aliases;
- former company names;
- dissolved companies;
- shell companies;
- DBAs;
- subsidiaries;
- shared addresses;
- shared officers;
- registered agents;
- trademarks;
- domains;
- merchant names;
- payment descriptors.

Goal: stop a network from appearing larger or more independent than it really is because the same principals operate through multiple names.

## 2. Domain / infrastructure history

For websites and shops, preserve:
- WHOIS history where available;
- domain registration dates;
- ownership/contact changes;
- hosting/CDN relationships;
- shared analytics IDs;
- shared ad pixels;
- shared email infrastructure;
- shared ecommerce infrastructure;
- historical redirects;
- archived site ownership claims.

Shared technical infrastructure alone is not proof of coordination, but it can reveal hidden relationships worth checking.

## 3. Corporate-address clustering

Search whether multiple supposedly independent companies use:
- same office;
- same virtual office;
- same mailbox;
- same registered agent;
- same accountant;
- same law firm;
- same fulfillment center.

Flag clusters for deeper review.

## 4. Payment-processor / merchant-of-record tracing

Where visible, identify:
- Stripe;
- PayPal;
- Shopify;
- ClickBank;
- Kajabi;
- Gumroad;
- Patreon;
- Substack;
- merchant-of-record companies;
- recurring-billing processors.

Record what appears on customer statements when known.

This may reveal that multiple "independent" sellers are routed through the same commercial operator.

## 5. Advertising-spend / lead-generation investigation

Look for:
- Meta Ad Library;
- Google Ads transparency;
- sponsored YouTube placements;
- newsletter cross-promotions;
- purchased email lists;
- lead magnets;
- webinar funnels;
- retargeting pixels;
- paid advertorials.

Question:
**How much audience growth is organic versus purchased marketing?**

## 6. Audience-transfer mapping

Track repeated movement of the same audience through:
- guest interviews;
- email swaps;
- conference invitations;
- referral links;
- joint livestreams;
- documentary appearances;
- bundle offers.

Goal:
identify who acts as a **traffic broker** for the ecosystem.

## 7. Customer-lifecycle / LTV reconstruction

For each commercial hub, estimate the sales ladder:

free video
→ email signup
→ low-cost report
→ subscription
→ premium membership
→ conference
→ VIP
→ consulting / high-ticket service

Estimate theoretical customer lifetime value only when assumptions are explicit.

## 8. Complaint-pattern normalization

Create a standardized complaint taxonomy:
- inaccurate forecasts;
- poor value;
- bait-and-switch;
- inaccessible promised content;
- refund refusal;
- cancellation difficulty;
- unauthorized recurring billing;
- poor support;
- defective device;
- scientific claims not supported;
- hidden affiliate relationship;
- product materially different from marketing;
- delivery failures;
- expensive information available freely elsewhere.

This allows comparisons across sellers.

## 9. Refund-policy history

Archive:
- refund terms;
- cancellation procedures;
- auto-renewal language;
- arbitration clauses;
- chargeback restrictions;
- "all sales final" terms.

Track changes over time.

## 10. Terms-of-service / legal-language audit

Look for contradictions between public marketing and fine print.

Examples:
- "highly accurate prediction system" in marketing vs "for entertainment/education only" in terms;
- strong health claims vs "not intended to diagnose/treat";
- "independent research" vs paid-client disclosures.

## 11. Testimonials audit

For testimonials:
- identify testimonial giver;
- determine whether customer is real;
- check whether person is employee/affiliate/friend;
- look for compensation;
- test whether quote was edited;
- identify reused stock photos;
- search identical text elsewhere.

## 12. Credential audit

For every expert:
- degree;
- institution;
- license;
- active/inactive status;
- disciplinary history;
- board certification;
- publication history;
- claimed government position;
- claimed intelligence/military role;
- claimed advisory role.

Separate:
**real credential** from **credential that does not support the claim being made**.

## 13. Government-consulting claim audit

Especially important for Armstrong-style claims.

For anyone claiming governments/central banks/intelligence agencies consult them:
- agency confirmation;
- contracts;
- procurement databases;
- meeting calendars;
- visitor logs;
- official event programs;
- testimony;
- FOIA;
- archived government references;
- named officials confirming relationship.

No confirmation = do not repeat as fact.

## 14. "Predicted it" retroactive-claim audit

Search for:
- original pre-event prediction;
- edited pages;
- changed dates;
- deleted posts;
- vague language later interpreted as specific;
- screenshots that omit original context;
- different predictions made simultaneously.

Maintain:
**original wording → later retelling → difference**

## 15. Failed-prediction cemetery

Create a permanent database only for clearly falsifiable misses.

This should be searchable by:
- person;
- date;
- topic;
- predicted outcome;
- deadline;
- actual outcome.

This prevents cherry-picking only successful calls.

## 16. Narrative mutation tracking

Track how one claim changes as it moves through the network.

Example structure:

original source
→ first interpretation
→ stronger paraphrase
→ podcast retelling
→ social-media headline
→ commercial pitch

Goal:
show exactly where uncertainty becomes certainty or a hypothesis becomes "fact."

## 17. Citation-laundering audit

Look for circular sourcing:

Person A cites B.
B cites C.
C cites A.

Or:
ten websites all cite the same original unsupported article.

Count one underlying source, not ten.

## 18. Screenshot / quote authenticity audit

For viral screenshots and quotes:
- locate original;
- verify timestamp;
- check cropping/context;
- detect edits;
- compare archived page;
- identify satire/parody.

## 19. Scientific-paper quality audit

For scientific evidence:
- peer reviewed?
- retracted?
- corrected?
- sample size?
- observational vs causal?
- preregistered?
- funding?
- conflicts?
- replicated?
- journal quality?
- statistical errors?
- consensus vs fringe interpretation?

## 20. Expert-network funding graph

Connect:
- funder;
- researcher;
- paper;
- conference;
- media appearance;
- product.

This may show whether "independent experts" are financially dependent on the same ecosystem.

## 21. Nonprofit / foundation financial audit

For nonprofits:
- Form 990;
- executive compensation;
- contractor payments;
- related-party payments;
- grants;
- donor-advised funds where visible;
- loans;
- travel;
- conference costs;
- media expenses.

Compare mission claims with spending.

## 22. Litigation / complaint timeline

For each major actor:
- criminal cases;
- civil suits;
- regulatory proceedings;
- bankruptcies;
- arbitration;
- defamation suits;
- settlements;
- injunctions;

## 23. Regulator-warning database

Search and normalize:

## 24. Product price-comparison engine

For every promoted product:

Question:

## 27. Device teardown / laboratory testing

For high-value questionable physical products, consider:
- teardown;

## 28. Product-certification authenticity

Verify:

- ISO certification;

## 29. Sponsorship concentration

Calculate what percentage of a show's sponsors come from:
- precious metals;
- supplements;
- preparedness;

- privacy/security;
- crypto.

This can reveal economic incentives even without proving deceptive intent.

## 30. Revenue-dependence analysis

Estimate whether a media platform's business depends on audiences remaining convinced that:

- conventional finance is unsafe;

## 31. Cross-promotion reciprocity score

Measure:

- B links A's newsletter;
- shared conference appearances;

## 32. "Independent expert" independence audit

## 35. Employee / former-insider research

Search for:

## 45. Repetition-after-debunk audit

Track whether a claim is repeated **after** strong contradictory evidence is public.

## 46. Source-quality behavior score

For each actor track:

- circular citations;

## 47. Advertisement disclosure compliance

## 48. Pricing-history / artificial-discount audit

Track:

## 49. Scarcity authenticity audit

For physical and digital products:
- was stock actually limited?
- did "last chance" repeat indefinitely?
- was the same report later sold again?

## 50. Final rebuttal test

Before publishing a major allegation, construct the strongest plausible defense.

Ask:
- what evidence would make our conclusion wrong?
- did we seek it?
- did we include it?

The strongest exposé is one that survives the target's best response.

# Final hardening layer — locked

These requirements are mandatory before publishing major findings.

## A. Source preservation and chain of custody

For every high-value source preserve, where lawful and practical:
- canonical URL;
- archive URL;
- publication/access date;
- author/publisher;
- local filename or artifact reference;
- cryptographic hash for downloaded documents;
- screenshot or PDF page reference when visual context matters;
- transcription provenance for audio/video.

If a source later changes or disappears, retain the earlier version and record the change.

## B. Source deduplication

Do not count syndicated copies, mirrors, press-release rewrites, or articles that merely repeat the same underlying source as independent corroboration.

Maintain an **evidentiary lineage** field:
original source → derivative source → commentary source.

The 50-source minimum means **50 distinct sources**, not 50 URLs.

## C. Entity alias registry

Maintain canonical IDs for:
- people;
- aliases;
- companies;
- DBAs;
- former company names;
- product brands;
- domains;
- shell/holding companies;
- nonprofit entities.

Every graph edge and money record must point to canonical IDs.

## D. Change detection

Track changes to:
- biographies;
- product claims;
- prices;
- refund terms;
- disclosures;
- sponsor lists;
- predictions;
- ownership statements;
- "Made in USA" language;
- medical/scientific claims.

Record before/after versions with dates.

## E. Publication gate

A major damaging claim may enter the final exposé as **fact** only if:
1. the evidence is proportionate to the seriousness of the allegation;
2. primary evidence has been sought;
3. the subject's best available rebuttal has been checked;
4. contradictory evidence has been recorded;
5. source lineage is known;
6. the claim has a confidence rating;
7. wording distinguishes allegation, inference, and adjudicated fact.

## F. Reproducibility packet

Every major finding should be reproducible by another researcher using:
- source IDs;
- URLs/archives;
- exact quotes or timestamp ranges;
- calculations;
- data transformations;
- graph edges;
- product records;
- money-flow records.

No important conclusion should depend on a private intuition that cannot be reconstructed from the repo.

## G. Confidence scale

Use:
- **VERY HIGH** — multiple strong independent/primary sources
- **HIGH** — strong source plus corroboration
- **MEDIUM** — credible but incomplete
- **LOW** — plausible lead, weak evidence
- **UNVERIFIED** — allegation/lead only

## H. False-positive control

For each investigation category, deliberately include examples of:
- a scary claim that turned out accurate;
- a product that actually performs as advertised;
- a relationship that looked suspicious but had an innocent explanation;
- a critic whose attack failed verification.

This calibrates the project and reduces confirmation bias.

## I. Legal / wording review

Before publishing claims involving:
- fraud;
- scam;
- manipulation;
- Ponzi scheme;
- criminal conduct;
- medical deception;
- undisclosed financial interest;
- securities misconduct;

verify whether there is:
- conviction;
- plea;
- regulator finding;
- court judgment;
- settlement;
- documented financial record;
- or only an allegation.

Use the strongest accurate term supported by the record.

## J. Evidence coverage dashboard

For every core entity show:
- total distinct sources;
- primary sources;
- independent journalism;
- original statements;
- customer/community sources;
- product records;
- money records;
- court/regulator records;
- unresolved allegations;
- rebuttals captured;
- publication readiness.

## K. Watchlist / update queue

Maintain a queue for:
- new products;
- new sponsors;
- new companies;
- deleted pages;
- new conferences;
- new predictions;
- regulatory actions;
- lawsuits;
- price changes;
- ownership changes.

The project should be able to update without rebuilding the whole investigation.

## L. Final objective

The project should not merely say that an ecosystem is bad.

It should be able to show, where supported:

**who said what → what evidence existed → what they sold → who owned/made it → who got paid → what conflicts were disclosed → whether the claim/product held up → how the story changed over time.**

That is now the locked publication standard.

# Network-wide scope clarification — locked

Doomandgloom is **not a Catherine Austin Fitts / Martin Armstrong project**.

They are seed nodes only.

Any person, company, nonprofit, media platform, conference, product, sponsor, manufacturer, researcher, funder, advertiser, investment vehicle, distributor, affiliate, or recurring collaborator becomes part of scope when a documented relationship connects it to the network.

## Expansion rule

A new entity enters the research graph when at least one of the following is documented:

- formal employment / ownership / board relationship
- money flow
- product ownership or manufacturing
- affiliate/referral relationship
- sponsorship
- recurring cross-promotion
- recurring media appearances
- recurring conference appearances
- shared research funding
- shared legal/PR/marketing infrastructure
- shared commercial vendor
- audience-transfer relationship
- repeated claim propagation
- securities / investment relationship
- nonprofit / grant relationship
- related-party transaction

Single interviews or one-off conference appearances remain weak ties unless strengthened by additional evidence.

## Research objective

The project should discover the network **outward from every node**, not merely outward from the original seeds.

Every newly discovered hub can spawn another discovery pass.
